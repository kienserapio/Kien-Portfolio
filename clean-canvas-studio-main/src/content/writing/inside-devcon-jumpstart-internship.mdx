*This is the first of a three-part series documenting my experience as a DEVCON Jumpstart intern across two tracks — Full Stack Developer & DEVCON Studios, then Product Design. If you’ve ever wondered what it’s really like on the inside, this one’s for you.*

They say every great story has a beginning, a middle, and a resolution. But in tech, it usually just feels like a series of bugs until something finally clicks.

For a long time, I felt like I was living in a movie I hadn’t auditioned for. Growing up, I wanted to be a filmmaker. I dreamed of the cinematic scale of Scorsese or the mind-bending structures of Christopher Nolan. But life had a different script. I couldn’t find an Arts and Design track for Senior High, so I chose STEM. I almost applied for Civil Engineering, but at the last second, I found myself enrolled in Computer Science at TUP Manila.

I was a **nobody** in my own field. I had a background in graphic design and UI, but looking at a blank IDE felt like staring at a foreign language without a dictionary. I saw it in my classmates, too — that quiet, shared anxiety of being lost. We were CS students, but we were all trying to find a path that wasn’t just following a syllabus that felt ten years out of date.

![Group selfie of tech students sharing a meal at a fast-food restaurant after a tech event](/writing/inside-devcon-jumpstart-internship/02.webp "My First Tech Event and Networking with Fellow Tech Students")

## The “Why” Behind Hitting Apply

I’ll be honest with you: I didn’t apply to DEVCON Jumpstart because it looked good on a resume. I applied because I felt a gap — not a skills gap exactly, but a *wisdom* gap. There’s a version of computer science school teaches you, and then there’s the version that actually happens in the industry. I wanted the second one.

I wanted to grow as a person and a tech individual. I wanted to learn how to lead, how to collaborate with people who are as passionate about the craft as I am, and how to pick up the kind of industry-standard knowledge that no university syllabus quite captures. And yes — I wanted to meet people. Real people building real things.

DEVCON, with its network of eleven chapters nationwide and its finger on the pulse of Philippine tech, seemed like the right room to walk into.

It was.

![Three DEVCON interns giving a thumbs up in front of the DEVCON logo at the office](/writing/inside-devcon-jumpstart-internship/03.webp)

## Two Tracks, One Journey, Zero Regrets

My journey was unusual. I didn’t just do one internship; I did two, back-to-back, under the same Cohort 3 umbrella.

From **September to December 2025**, I was a **Full Stack Developer & DEVCON Studios Intern**. Then, from **January to April 2026**, I pivoted into **Product Design**. This wasn’t just a change in job title; it was a 360-degree view of how a tech organization actually breathes.

One day I was writing backend logic; the next, I was obsessing over the spacing of a single button in Figma. It taught me that whether you’re writing a script or setting up a shot, you’re trying to solve a problem for a human being. In tech, the code is the engine, but the design is the steering wheel. You need to understand both to drive. Switching tracks mid-cohort gave me the kind of versatility that you can’t get from a textbook. It forced me to be a generalist and a specialist at the same time, learning how the infrastructure supports the experience.

![DEVCON interns and volunteers posing outdoors at dusk with palm trees behind them](/writing/inside-devcon-jumpstart-internship/04.webp)

## Level 1: The Full Stack & Studios Grind

My first act began as a **Full Stack Developer & DEVCON Studios Intern**. This was the tutorial phase where the difficulty spike was real. I wasn’t just writing code; I was learning the operational machinery that keeps the tech world turning.

A huge part of this level was handling **Caffeine AI Manila Hackathon 2025**. This wasn’t just a coding task — it was a logistics battle. I attended three different alignment meeting briefings before the big day, fully supported by ICP, to ensure every gear was turning. When the event finally hit, I was responsible for the entire volunteer workforce. Managing people in a high-pressure environment is exactly like a real-time strategy game; you have to deploy resources, manage morale, and solve bottlenecks in seconds.

On the Studios side, I went back to my storytelling roots. I created carousel slides and social media content that led to a **60% increase** in our engagement. I started producing short-form interview content that pulled back the curtain on the behind-the-scenes lives of tech professionals and students. This was my first real taste of the **0 to 1** mindset — creating a content pipeline where there was none before. I learned that tech isn’t just about the build; it’s about how you communicate the value of that build to the world.

![DEVCON interns and mentors in a high-rise meeting room during a discussion](/writing/inside-devcon-jumpstart-internship/05.webp)

## Level 2: The Design & Build Phase Pivot

Then came the mid-game twist. I pivoted into **Product Design**. In game dev terms, I switched my character class from a Warrior to a Mage. I shifted from the how of the backend to the why of the user experience. But this wasn’t a “soft” transition — this is where I handled the biggest technical builds of my journey.

I was tasked with breathing life into the **DEVCON+ R&D Prototype** and building out the **WordPress Brizy** web properties. This was the moment I realized that being a designer in the real world means being a builder who understands people.

One of my biggest contributions was the **DEVCON+ UI Library**. I spent hours in Figma, but it wasn’t about making things look pretty; it was about **scalability**. I was architecting a system that had to be robust enough to serve eleven chapters nationwide. I learned the hard way that if a component isn’t reusable, it’s broken.

**The Dos and Don’ts of the Development Phase**

I learned that the biggest **Don’t** in tech is working in a vacuum. You can build the most beautiful prototype in the world, but if it doesn’t align with the developer’s reality or the user’s need, it’s just digital trash. The biggest **Do**? Documentation. I learned that your future self (and your team) will thank you more for a clear design system and a clean file structure than for a flashy animation.

Whether I was configuring WordPress sites or chaining AI tools to speed up the R&D process, I realized that the discipline is the same: you are trying to make the complex feel simple. I learned to treat every pixel with the same logic I treated every line of code.

## The Montage: When the Stakes Become Real

If this were a film, this is the part where the music picks up and the scenes blur together. On-site days at the Amihan office had a rhythm. You’d walk in and suddenly realize you weren’t doing a practice run. The projects were live. The stakes were actual.

The variety of work was staggering. One week I was using **Buffer** to push marketing operations at light speed; the next, I was traveling to **Dagupan, Pangasinan** for the **DEVCON Hour of AI at DOST RSTW 2026**. Standing there, seeing our work impact people on a national scale, I realized I wasn’t just a student anymore. I was part of the engine.

I was back to my filmmaking roots, but with a tech advocacy. I was the documentation volunteer at **QCX**, **Caffeine AI**, and **SHEisDEVCON**, interviewing industry pros like **Sir Harrison from SUI**. I wasn’t just taking photos; I was capturing the story of a community. I saw the logistics, the partner events, and the controlled chaos of a 300-person hackathon. I was building a network that no classroom could ever provide. Everything I wouldn’t have learned without DEVCON Philippines.

## The Book That Reframed Everything: Zero to One

Every Jumpstart intern reads a book. Mine was Peter Thiel’s **Zero to One**. It hit me differently because I was reading it while actively building.

Thiel’s central argument is simple: most progress is horizontal (**1 to n**) — copying what works and scaling it. But real innovation is vertical (**0 to 1**) — going from nothing to something entirely new. He argues that the goal of any serious company should be to build a creative monopoly: something so uniquely good that no one else offers a real substitute.

As an intern, this framework changed how I saw my own role. The default expectation of an intern is horizontal behavior — follow the process, execute the brief, don’t rock the boat. Thiel’s logic pushed me to ask: **what is the one problem in this office that nobody is looking at?** If you find it and solve it, you don’t just add value — you create a kind of monopoly on that specific value.

He asks a terrifying question:

> **What important truth do very few people agree with you on?**

As a junior dev, that quote became my permission slip. It gave me the courage to stop seeking the safety of consensus and start trusting my own perception. I started hunting for office secrets — those little inefficiencies everyone accepted as normal but were actually broken. I tried to build proprietary knowledge — to become the person who understood a system so well that I wasn’t just a swap-in intern, but a vital part of the production.

## The Verdict: A Journey Worth Sharing

I’ve been asked to rate this experience, and I give it a **9 out of 10**.

The missing 1 isn’t a flaw; it’s the realness of the industry. It represents the moments where project management was messy or where I wished I had more time to dive even deeper into my track. But that gap is where the growth happened. A **10/10** is a fairy tale; a **9/10** is a journey that actually changed me.

What I gained — the connections, the mentorship, the immeasurable experiences outside the main deliverables — is what changed my identity. I went from being a nobody from TUP Manila to someone who has lead real projects and can hold a conversation with a tech lead without flinching. I’ve realized that the most important thing you build isn’t a website or an app — it’s the story you’re able to share with the people coming up behind you.

## To Every Lost Tech Student

Every happy journey has to end. As my time in Cohort 3 wraps up, the feeling is bittersweet — melancholic, like the last day of a film set where the crew has become family.

To the tech students at TUP Manila, or any university, who feel lost: I see you. I was the kid who wanted to be an art student but ended up in a Computer Science lab. I was the one who thought he started too late.

But here is the truth: Your story has already started. Whether you’re a designer, a writer, a volunteer, or a coder, your unique background is your **0 to 1.** Don’t wait for the syllabus to give you permission to be great. The room is worth walking into. The **wisdom gap** is exactly the thing worth closing.

I didn’t just learn how to use Claude Code or Figma; I learned why I embarked on this tech journey in the first place. I found my tech advocacy. I found a way to bridge my love for storytelling with the power of software.

The credits are rolling on this internship, but for the first time, I’m not worried about the next role. I know how to find the next story. And if a “nobody” from Quezon City can find his soul in the middle of a tech internship, **so can you.**

**Apply now and start your profound journey with DEVCON.**
