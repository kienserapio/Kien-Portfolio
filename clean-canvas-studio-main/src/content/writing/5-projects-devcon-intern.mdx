> “The best way to predict the future is to invent it.”

> — **Alan Kay**

In software engineering, there is a core concept called **Optimization**. It isn’t about teaching a system how to work — the logic is already there — it’s about making it run at peak efficiency without breaking a sweat. It’s the difference between a script that barely finishes and a production-grade system that scales to thousands.

Before the internship, I had a particular kind of résumé. I had placed in game jams — built playable prototypes in 48 and 72 hours, game loops from scratch, shipped mechanics that worked under deadline pressure when most teams were still arguing about scope. I had competed in hackathons and won. I knew what it felt like to take a brief, decompose it into a technical architecture, and deliver something functional before the clock ran out. I thought that background was my edge. And it was — but it was an edge that only cut in one direction. What I didn’t know yet was how to build for organizations. For communities. For systems that outlive the sprint. DEVCON changed that. This is the record of how.

What followed was the most professionally formative season of my career so far. Five distinct projects. Five completely different disciplines. One throughline: the belief that great software, great events, and great stories are all the product of the same underlying skill — the ability to understand a system, respect its constraints, and design something trustworthy inside them. This is the record of everything I learned. If you’re looking for a portfolio filled with school assignments and “Hello World” repositories, you’re in the wrong place. This is **Proof of Work**. In the blockchain space, this is the cryptographic evidence of effort. In the professional world, it’s the difference between saying you’re a developer and proving you can ship under fire.

This is a deep dive into the five projects that moved me from being a high-performing student at **Technological University of the Philippines — Manila** to a professional builder who treats every project like a glimpse of a successful product that will shape the lives of people.

## The Core Philosophy

To understand these projects, you have to understand the three-step validation process I applied to every deliverable:

## 1. The 0 to 1 Audit (Ideation Phase)

Before writing a single line of code or drawing a frame, I identify the **“Office Secret”** — the inefficiency everyone accepts as normal but is actually broken. This is where the strategy is born. We aren’t just building for the sake of building; we’re solving for a specific, identified friction.

## 2. The Orchestration (Build Phase)

This is the “Work” in Proof of Work. It involves chaining AI tools, designing robust component libraries in Figma, and ensuring that the developer-designer handoff is seamless. It’s about managing the stack to ensure the output is greater than the sum of its parts.

## 3. The Stress Test (Deployment Phase)

A project isn’t done when it’s shipped; it’s done when it **survives the user**. Whether it’s a 300-person hackathon or a national UI library, the evidence is in the stability. If it breaks under pressure, the “work” hasn’t been proven yet.

## 01. Caffeine AI Manila — Event Volunteer Lead

There’s a specific feeling that comes with being handpicked for something. It’s not just pride — it’s the weight of expectation landing on your shoulders and you choosing to carry it rather than shrug it off. That’s what Caffeine AI Manila was for me. I wasn’t assigned this project because my name was next on a list. I was selected because the people running **DEVCON** believed, based on everything they’d seen of my work, that I was the right person to be trusted with it. That trust was the **most motivating thing** I’d ever been handed — and the most demanding.

The scope was broader than any single title could capture. Yes, I was the Event Volunteer Lead for the first-ever Caffeine **AI Hackathon in the Philippines** — a 300+ attendee event with over 200 active hackers, a 15-person volunteer team, and the kind of moving-parts complexity that makes your coordination instincts either sharpen or break. But the scope extended far beyond the event floor. I was also developing the Caffeine AI digital presence: building and configuring the websites that would serve as the public face of the platform, and building games designed specifically to test and showcase a new product from **ICP Philippines** that had already gone live. These weren’t separate assignments. They were one integrated mission: make Caffeine AI real, credible, and compelling — in both the physical and digital space.

![Large crowd of Caffeine AI Manila attendees posing together under the Caffeine sign](/writing/5-projects-devcon-intern/04.webp)

The **competitive audit work** was where my developer brain and my strategic instincts collided most productively. Before a single line of code was written or a single volunteer briefed, I needed to understand the landscape. What were Caffeine AI’s competitors doing well? Where were their gaps? What did the market positioning look like, and where did Caffeine AI’s strengths actually live relative to what was already out there? I approached the audit systematically: I documented feature sets, analyzed UX flows, mapped value propositions, and evaluated brand positioning across comparable platforms. The output wasn’t a mood board — it was a structured analysis with identified differentiators and a clear articulation of where Caffeine AI had genuine competitive advantage. That document shaped both the website copy I wrote and the game mechanics I designed, because both of them needed to communicate the platform’s identity, not just its existence.

On the event floor itself, the **student leadership skills** I had been building over years of campus involvement came into full operational deployment. Leading 15 volunteers across 300+ attendees is not a logistics problem. It is a communication architecture problem. I built a tiered coordination structure before the event: frontline volunteers owned their domains, escalation paths were defined before they were needed, and decision-critical calls funneled to me. The event floor became a distributed system in my mind, and my job was to monitor its health in real time and intervene before failures became visible to participants. Two role reassignments happened on the fly. Zero of those decisions showed up in the experience of the people who came to build. That invisibility was the win. What I took away from Caffeine AI Manila wasn’t a single skill — it was the confirmation that a developer who can also lead, analyze, and communicate is an entirely different kind of asset than a developer who can only code.

## 03. DEVCON+ UI Design — From a Sketch to a System

The brief was this: **design a full component library and design system in Figma for a platform serving all 11 DEVCON chapters nationwide**. The platform needed to handle member verification, event check-ins, volunteer management, and chapter-level administration. Eleven chapters. Hundreds of volunteers. Participants who had no training on the software and no patience for learning curves. A system that had to be so intuitive it disappeared — that had to get out of the way and let the community happen. That was the constraint. Everything else was a design decision.

I started where every serious UI process should start: not in Figma, but in **understanding**. Before a single frame was created, I needed to know who I was designing for. I built out user personas for the three primary actors in the **DEVCON+ ecosystem**: the chapter participant (tech-curious, event-motivated, arriving with social energy and zero appetite for software friction), the volunteer coordinator (operating under social pressure, managing multiple moving parts, needing confidence in the interface before they can extend confidence to participants), and the chapter administrator (technically comfortable, managing data, requiring precision and audit-ability over aesthetics). Each persona had a distinct relationship with the interface, and those distinctions drove every subsequent decision. Design without personas is decoration. Design with them is architecture.

The process followed a formal progression that I had studied but never executed at this scale:

**Sketches → Low-Fidelity Wireframes → Mid-Fidelity Prototypes → Final Prototype Design.**

The sketch phase was deliberately analog — pen and paper, not Figma. I find that working on screen too early anchors you to the constraints of the tool instead of the freedom of the problem. Sketches let you think faster than you can execute, which means the bad ideas get discarded before they ever waste a single frame of build time. The low-fidelity wireframes in Figma were about structure: information hierarchy, layout logic, navigation flow. No colors, no typography choices, no component styling — just the bones.

The UI principles I leaned on most heavily were not the ones typically taught in design courses. Visual hierarchy and color theory are table stakes — they matter, but they’re learnable in an afternoon. The principles that actually shaped DEVCON+ were the ones that live at the intersection of psychology and engineering. **Hick’s Law** governed every decision about option density: the more choices a volunteer coordinator sees at a critical moment, the longer their decision time, and in an event environment where a line is forming behind them, latency is failure. **Fitts’s Law** informed the sizing and placement of primary action targets — check-in buttons are large, centered, and impossible to miss even on a small screen at arm’s length. The **Principle of Progressive Disclosure** shaped how I layered information in the admin interfaces: show only what’s needed for the current task, and reveal complexity on demand rather than by default. These aren’t aesthetic preferences. They are design decisions with measurable outcomes.

The component architecture was built on **atomic design methodology.** Every element in the library was categorized as an atom, molecule, organism, template, or page — and the rule was that nothing at a higher level could be built except from components at the level below it. An atom: a color chip, a typographic style, a spacing unit, a border radius token. A molecule: a labeled input field combining an atom label, an atom text input, and an atom error state. An organism: a registration form composing multiple molecules with defined validation logic. This discipline is what gave the system its extensibility. When a new chapter feature required a new UI pattern, the answer was almost always “compose existing atoms” rather than “create a new component.” The system grew without fragmenting.

**Google Stitch** entered the workflow as the critical bridge between design and implementation — and understanding why this matters requires understanding the problem it solves. Design-to-development handoffs are where UI integrity goes to die. A designer exports screens, a developer eyeballs the spacing, interprets the colors, guesses at the border radius — and three weeks into development, the product looks like a rough approximation of the design rather than the design itself. Stitch eliminated that gap for DEVCON+. By extracting CSS variables and design tokens directly from my Figma variables — color-primary-500, spacing-md, radius-sm, shadow-level-2 — and mapping them to production-ready CSS, Stitch created a living contract between the design system and the codebase. When I updated a token in Figma, the change propagated. The design system wasn’t a deliverable that got handed off and forgotten. It was a live source of truth.

What gave me an edge in this process that a pure UI designer wouldn’t have had was the ability to **design with the implementation in mind**. When I structured the component hierarchy in Figma, I was also thinking about the **React component tree** that would eventually mirror it. When I defined interaction states — hover, focus, active, disabled, error, loading — I was thinking about the CSS state selectors and the conditional rendering logic that would produce them. Designing without understanding implementation is like writing an essay in a language you don’t speak. You can follow the grammar, but you can’t hear the rhythm. Understanding both gave me the ability to make design decisions that weren’t just visually correct — **they were architecturally sound.**

## 04. The DEVCON Web Ecosystem — Building with WordPress & Brizy

Three websites. Three audiences. One developer. The DEVCON web ecosystem — **SHEisDEVCON, the Global site, and the Jumpstart internship portal** — sounds like a production sprint when you describe it that way. In practice, it was an exercise in something I’ll call tone engineering: the discipline of making a technical medium feel emotionally specific to the human on the other side of the screen.

My workflow for each site started in **Figma**, not Brizy. I had found the [**Brizy UI Web Kit**](https://www.figma.com/community/file/1336729660393386003/brizy-web-ui-kit) on the Figma Community page — a comprehensive kit of pre-built Brizy components, layouts, and sections that I could use to prototype each site’s structure and visual language before ever opening the CMS. This was a deliberate process decision, and it made an enormous difference in output quality. Prototyping in Figma first meant that client-facing decisions — layout structure, section ordering, content hierarchy, visual tone — were resolved before I was working in a production environment where changes are slower and more expensive to reverse. The Figma prototype was the contract. Brizy was the execution.

**SHEisDEVCON** demanded warmth and credibility in equal measure. The user persona here is a woman in tech — potentially arriving with skepticism about whether this space is genuinely built for her, or whether it’s another well-meaning initiative that centers the wrong things. Every visual and structural decision I made had to answer that skepticism before she consciously formed it. Typography choices were approachable without being frivolous. The color palette was grounded and confident rather than soft and apologetic. Hero sections centered faces and stories, not logos and statistics. The Figma prototype let me test those choices against the persona before I committed to them in Brizy, which meant the final site was a resolved design, not an iterated guess.

The Global site required the opposite register. International audiences and potential organizational partners evaluate credibility through different signals — grid discipline, information density, restrained visual language, and the absence of anything that reads as regional or informal. The Brizy UI Web Kit gave me the layout primitives I needed; the Figma prototype gave me a place to resolve the tone before it became a production decision. The Jumpstart internship portal needed to feel like an invitation — a sense of momentum and forward motion that a prospective intern would feel in their chest before they read a single word about the program. Three different emotional architectures, three different Figma prototypes, three different Brizy builds. One consistent principle: you design for the human, not the medium.

## 05. DEVCON+ Beta R&D Prototype — Building a Product with AI as Your Engineering Team

This is the section I’ve thought most carefully about how to write. Not because the work was the most technically complex I’ve done — though it was — but because it represents something I believe is genuinely important for every developer reading this to understand: the difference between using AI and orchestrating AI. Using AI is asking a chatbot to fix your bug. Orchestrating AI is designing a multi-layer pipeline where language models, design tools, scaffolding engines, and backend infrastructure each occupy a defined role in a software development lifecycle — and understanding exactly what you’re asking of each layer, why, and what you do when it fails. That is what I built for DEVCON+. And here is the full technical case study of how.

**The Problem Space**

**DEVCON+** is not a simple application. It is a community platform serving eleven chapters across the Philippines, each with its own membership base, event cadence, and administrative needs. The system needs to handle member onboarding and verification, event creation and management, QR-based check-in for participants, volunteer role management, chapter-level data isolation, and a permission model complex enough to distinguish between a participant, a volunteer, a chapter administrator, and a super-admin — while remaining simple enough that a first-time volunteer can use it confidently on the first try. Building this as a solo developer, on an intern’s timeline, without sacrificing design integrity or architectural soundness, was the challenge. The only way I saw to meet it was to treat AI as a full-cycle engineering partner, not a productivity tool.

Before any tool was opened, before any prompt was written, I did the thing that most developers skip because it feels slow: I defined the problem thoroughly. I mapped the existing DEVCON operational workflows. I interviewed chapter coordinators about their pain points with the current system. I catalogued legacy documentation and previous feature requests. I built a structured overview of every user type in the system, their goals, their constraints, and their tolerance for complexity. This phase felt like administrative overhead. It was actually the most important engineering work I did — because everything downstream, every AI-generated output, every schema decision, every UI pattern, was only as good as the clarity I established here.

**Phase One: Requirements Engineering with NotebookLM and Gemini**

The first tool in the pipeline was NotebookLM, and I want to be precise about why I chose it and what I used it for — because “I used NotebookLM to generate PRDs” is a description that understates the actual process significantly.

**NotebookLM** is, at its core, a grounded **AI reasoning tool** — it synthesizes information from sources you provide rather than generating from its parametric training data. That distinction is critical for software requirements engineering. A language model generating requirements from nothing produces generic, vibe-coded specs that describe software in the abstract rather than the specific. NotebookLM **generating requirements from the raw community data** I fed it — chapter coordinator interview notes, legacy documentation, participant feedback forms, event post-mortems — produced requirements grounded in the actual operational reality of DEVCON as an organization. I sourced it heavily. The output quality was a direct function of the input quality, which means the document work I did in the pre-phase was not overhead — it was engineering.

![Claude chat prompt asking for a complete PRD for the DEVCON+ member management platform](/writing/5-projects-devcon-intern/16.webp)

![Claude response summarizing the generated DEVCON+ PRD sections with a downloadable DOCX file](/writing/5-projects-devcon-intern/17.webp)

The output of the NotebookLM analysis phase was fed into **Gemini and Claude** **for PRD synthesis.** I prompted Claude to generate **structured Product Requirement Documents** following a format I specified: Executive Summary, User Stories organized by persona, Functional Requirements with acceptance criteria, Non-Functional Requirements (performance, security, accessibility), and Technical Constraints. Each user story followed the standard format — “As a \[user type\], I want to \[action\], so that \[outcome\]” — with acceptance criteria specific enough to serve as a QA checklist. The non-functional requirements section included response time targets (sub-200ms for check-in confirmation on event day), concurrent user capacity estimates based on chapter event data, and accessibility requirements aligned to WCAG 2.1 AA standards.

The PRDs that came out of this process were not supplementary documentation. They were the engineering contract for the entire build. Every subsequent decision — database schema, UI component behavior, API design — was referenced against the PRD to ensure traceability. When a feature seemed like a good idea during development, the first question was always: is this in the PRD? If yes, build it. If no, flag it, document it as a proposed change, and evaluate it against the original requirements. This discipline — treating the PRD as a living source of truth rather than a document that gets written and forgotten — is what separates professional software development from freestyle coding. I had never worked this way before. I will work this way on every project I build from here.

**Phase Two: Design System Architecture and Token Extraction with Google Stitch**

With the PRDs established, the next phase was design — and because the DEVCON+ design system had already been built in Figma during the UI Design project, the foundation was in place. What this phase required was translating that design system into a format that could be consumed by the development environment without human error in the handoff.

![Google Stitch canvas showing a row of DEVCON+ mobile dashboard screen designs](/writing/5-projects-devcon-intern/18.webp)

The design token architecture I had established in Figma used a semantic naming convention across four categories: color tokens (color-primary, color-secondary, color-surface, color-error, color-success, with numeric scale values 100 through 900), spacing tokens (spacing-xs through spacing-2xl, based on a 4px base unit), typographic tokens (font-size, font-weight, line-height, letter-spacing), and component tokens (radius, shadow, border). Semantic naming is a deliberate choice: a token called color-primary-500 communicates intent and position in the scale; a token called #1A6BFF communicates a raw value that becomes meaningless the moment the brand color changes. Semantic tokens make design systems resilient to change.

**Google Stitch** consumed the Figma design token library and generated a CSS variables file that mapped every token name to its computed value. The output was a single :root block in CSS that became the single source of truth for the entire frontend. Every component, every layout, every interactive state in the application referenced a CSS variable rather than a hardcoded value. This means that a brand color update, a spacing scale adjustment, or a typographic change could be made in one place — the Figma token library — propagated through Stitch, and applied to the entire application in a single deployment. The design-to-code pipeline was not a one-time handoff. It was a continuous synchronization system.

**Phase Three: Frontend Scaffolding with Lovable**

This is the phase that most developers would skip — generating a scaffold from an AI tool and then refactoring it feels, to a certain kind of developer ego, like admitting you couldn’t build it yourself. That framing is wrong, and I want to address it directly.

![Lovable editor previewing the DEVCON+ mobile onboarding screen "The Heart of Philippine Tech"](/writing/5-projects-devcon-intern/19.webp)

***The original Lovable Frontend prototype:***[*https://devconplusrndprototype.lovable.app*](https://devconplusrndprototype.lovable.app/)

Software engineering is not about demonstrating that you can build every component from scratch. It is about delivering working, maintainable, scalable software as efficiently as possible. A scaffold is not a crutch — it is a starting point with more structure than a blank file and more context-specificity than a generic template. **Lovable,** given the PRD context and the design token system, produced a frontend scaffold with a component structure, a routing architecture, a basic state management pattern, and a set of initial page layouts that I could evaluate, critique, and refactor. That process — refactoring a scaffold — is genuinely different from building from scratch, and in a specific way that’s worth understanding: when you start with a scaffold, you’re in dialogue with a system. You can see its assumptions, challenge them, and replace them with better ones. When you start with a blank file, you’re in a monologue.

The scaffold **Lovable** **generated used React** with functional components and hooks, which aligned with my architectural intent. What it got wrong — consistently, in ways that were predictable — was the separation of concerns. The initial scaffold conflated UI state with server state, mixed data fetching logic directly into component bodies, and used inline styles in places where the design token CSS variables should have been referenced. The refactoring pass was systematic: I extracted all data fetching into custom hooks using a pattern that separated the query logic from the component rendering logic. I replaced every hardcoded style value with the corresponding CSS variable from the Stitch-generated token file. I restructured the component hierarchy to match the atomic design structure established in Figma — ensuring that the development component tree mirrored the design component tree, which makes future design updates directly traceable to code changes.

**Phase Four: Business Logic, Backend Architecture, and Claude Code**

This is where the build became genuinely complex, and where **Claude Code became the most important tool in the pipeline**. Business logic is the part of a software system that most directly encodes the organization’s rules, workflows, and edge cases — and for DEVCON+, those rules were numerous, interdependent, and consequential.

![Supabase schema visualizer showing DEVCON+ tables for jobs, rewards, and point transactions](/writing/5-projects-devcon-intern/20.webp)

The backend was built on **Supabase**, which serves as both the **PostgreSQL database and the authentication infrastructure.** The schema design started from the PRD’s functional requirements and entity relationship analysis: Users, Chapters, Events, Registrations, CheckIns, VolunteerRoles, and Memberships as the primary tables, with junction tables handling the many-to-many relationships between users and chapters, users and events, and volunteers and roles. Foreign key constraints were defined explicitly, cascade behaviors were specified for deletion operations, and indexed columns were identified based on the query patterns defined in the PRD — specifically the check-in lookup (by QR code value and event ID) and the membership verification query (by user ID and chapter ID), both of which needed to resolve in under 200 milliseconds on event day with concurrent load.

The **Row Level Security (RLS) policies** were the most architecturally demanding part of the backend work. RLS in PostgreSQL is a feature that enforces access control at the database level rather than the application level — every SQL query executed against a Supabase table is automatically filtered by the RLS policy defined for that table, based on the authenticated user’s identity and claims. For DEVCON+, the RLS model needed to enforce chapter-level data isolation: a chapter administrator for DEVCON Manila should be able to read and write data for Manila events and members, but not for DEVCON Cebu. A participant should be able to read their own registration data but not modify it. A super-admin should have unrestricted access across all chapters. I implemented this using a combination of Supabase auth claims and a custom roles table, with RLS policies expressed as PostgreSQL security policy expressions that joined against the roles table to evaluate the requesting user’s permissions in context. Getting this right required understanding both the PostgreSQL security model and the Supabase auth flow at a depth that went well beyond what the documentation covers at the introductory level.

**Claude Code’s role in this phase was refactoring the business logic layer** — the TypeScript service functions, custom React hooks, and API utility modules that sit between the UI and the database. The initial implementations worked. They were not clean. Claude Code helped me refactor toward patterns that would scale: centralizing error handling in a typed error class hierarchy, standardizing the async data fetching pattern across all service modules, extracting the Supabase client initialization into a singleton to prevent connection pool fragmentation, and restructuring the authentication flow to handle token refresh, session persistence, and unauthenticated state transitions correctly. The value Claude Code provided was not the generation of novel code — it was the systematic application of engineering best practices to code that already functionally existed. It was the difference between code that works and code that a team can maintain.

## The 5 Lessons That Actually Stuck

Lessons from an internship usually sound like platitudes. These are not platitudes. These are specific things I learned, used, and will keep using to bridge the gap between student projects and industry products.

**1\. Ideation is the Engine; Tech is the Fuel.** Without a solid why and a clear vision, even the most advanced AI is just spinning its wheels. I learned that the technologies we use won't be able to do the work—or even begin properly—if the foundational idea isn't architected first. You don't start with code; you start with the problem you are obsessed with solving.

**2\. PRDs are Architecture for AI (NotebookLM + Gemini).** Documentation is no longer just for humans. I used **NotebookLM and Gemini** to synthesize community feedback and legacy data into structured **Product Requirement Documents (PRDs)**. I realized that a well-built PRD acts as the context for an AI coder. If your documentation is architecturally sound, you’re giving your AI agents a blueprint they can actually execute against without hallucinating.

**3\. Semantic Fidelity is the New Handoff (Google Stitch + Figma).** The traditional design-to-dev gap is usually a language gap. By using **Google Stitch** to pull CSS variables and design tokens directly from **Figma**, I stopped giving the AI vibes and started giving it data. Providing an AI agent with structured design context instead of just screenshots is the secret to moving from a mockup to a functional UI with near-zero friction.

**4\. Claude Code: From Chatbot to Agentic Collaborator.** The difference between a chatbot that helps you write code and an agent that reasons about your codebase is the difference between a calculator and a collaborator. Having hands-on access to **Claude Code** at DEVCON was a game-changer. I moved from copy-pasting snippets to **Agentic Engineering**—giving an agent terminal access to refactor logic and manage state across an entire repository. Once you see a tool reason about a whole system, you can’t unsee it.

**5\. The Human Heart Behind It All.** The most durable lesson wasn't technical at all: it’s that technology is only as powerful as the passion driving it. While AI can handle the how, humans must provide the why. My technical output was fueled by soft skills—communication with my heads, collaboration with my co-interns, and the grit to push an idea through. Passion is the spark that keeps the R&D pipeline from becoming a chaos machine. We don’t just build systems; we build comfort for people.

## The One Skill That Ties It All Together

If I had to pin down the single most important thing I’m taking away from this, it isn’t a new language or a fancy framework. It’s the realization that my role in this industry has fundamentally changed.

I stopped being a **coder** and started being an **orchestrator**.

Before DEVCON, I saw AI as a high-tech calculator — something to help me finish a task faster. But after seeing it in the wild, I realized that speed without structure is just a faster way to fail. I went from the traditional, manual grind to leading a symphony of intelligent tools. I learned that the human work — **the PRDs, the user flows, the architectural vision** — is the only thing that keeps the mad scientist workflow from turning into a disaster.

The student who started this internship was looking for technical skills. The developer finishing it has found a new way to see the world. I learned how to look at a chaotic mess of tools and see a path through them. That’s something a syllabus can’t give you, and it’s something I’ll never lose.
