## The Question Nobody’s Asking Right

Every time AI comes up in a room full of tech students or early-career developers, the conversation collapses into the same exhausting binary: *Will AI take my job, or won’t it?*

I think that’s the wrong question entirely. And my time at DEVCON Jumpstart — actually building with these tools, not just theorizing about them — has made me more certain of that than ever.

The current discourse around AI is trapped in a dangerous binary: either AI is a “tool” that assists us, or a “threat” that replaces us. My hot take is that both framings miss the point. AI is a **fundamental shift in the human condition** — and the real danger isn’t the technology. The real danger is what happens to the people who refuse to meet it honestly.

![NotebookLM infographic titled "PRD to MVP: The AI-Native Pipeline" comparing AI-native and legacy workflows](/writing/ai-wont-make-you-obsolete/03.webp)

## The Tools I Actually Used

Before the philosophy, the specifics. During my internship, the primary AI tools I worked with were **Claude** and **Gemini** — both for different things and in different contexts.

Claude, and specifically **Claude Code**, was the AI I used most deeply. There’s a meaningful difference between using an AI to generate code snippets and using an AI agent that reasons about your codebase, understands the architecture, and helps you make structural decisions. Claude Code sits firmly in the second category. That’s why DEVCON uses it organization-wide, and that’s why learning to use it well was one of the most valuable things I did here.

**Gemini** played a supporting role across research and drafting workflows — a complement to the pipeline rather than the center of it.

The most interesting thing I built with these tools was a custom R&D prototype using a chained pipeline: **NotebookLM → Google Stitch → Lovable → Claude**. Design context in, working Supabase-connected application out. It was proof that a single developer, properly equipped, can build things that previously required a team.

## Wisdom Over Output: Why the Human Still Matters

Here’s the truth that the “AI will replace us” narrative always misses: an AI, no matter how sophisticated, is a **stochastic mirror**. It reflects what you bring to it. It generates output; it cannot generate intent.

Without the critical judgment of the person using it, AI is just noise — statistically coherent noise, sometimes impressively structured noise, but noise. It can write code, but it cannot understand *why* a community needs a specific feature. It can generate a design, but it cannot feel the friction that a real user experiences.

The magic was never in the model. The magic is in the human orchestration of the model.

During my internship, I experienced this directly. When I built the DEVCON+ R&D prototype, the value I added wasn’t in running the pipeline — it was in knowing *why* a certain architecture was better, *what* the business logic needed to accomplish, and *where* the AI was generating plausible-but-wrong outputs that I needed to catch and correct. That’s a human skill. It’s not going anywhere.

## Ethics as the First Principle

Here’s the piece of this conversation that I think gets skipped over most dangerously: **we cannot adapt to AI effectively if we don’t adapt to it ethically.**

Moving faster with AI tools without an ethical compass isn’t efficiency — it’s a liability. And the risks are real: intellectual property violations in AI-assisted art, data privacy gaps in AI-powered engineering, algorithmic bias in AI-driven social systems.

I believe we have a duty to understand the mechanics of these systems before we exploit their outputs. Not because it’s a legal obligation (though sometimes it is), but because the people on the other side of everything we build deserve that care. The users of DEVCON+. The volunteers whose data flows through the systems I designed. The developers whose work is ingested by the models I use.

To truly master AI, you have to first be honest about its edges — what it doesn’t know, what it gets wrong, what it reinforces thoughtlessly if you aren’t paying attention.

Responsibility grows in proportion to reach. The faster and more powerful our tools become, the more carefully we have to think about what we’re doing with them.

## The “Cyborg” Workflow vs. Stagnation

During my internship — and especially during projects like building the DEVCON+ prototype — I arrived at a description for the kind of work I was doing: the **“Cyborg” Workflow**.

Not human-only. Not AI-only. Human intent, machine reach.

The concrete version of this looked like: I handled strategic architecture and empathy. Claude handled the implementation at speed. I caught the errors. I made the calls that required understanding the real-world context. The AI carried the weight of the boilerplate, the refactoring, the pattern-matching.

By using Claude Code and Google Stitch as orchestrated parts of a system rather than isolated helpers, I didn’t work less — I worked *better*. My attention went to the problems that AI genuinely cannot touch: the design decisions that required understanding what volunteers actually feel when they’re checking someone into an event, the content decisions that required knowing what kind of story Mykiell’s journey deserved to be told as.

The alternative — refusing to learn these tools, insisting on doing everything the old way out of principle or fear — isn’t integrity. It’s stagnation. And stagnation, in a field moving at this pace, is a choice with consequences.

## The Question Worth Asking Instead

Stop asking: *“Will AI take my job?”*

Start asking: **“How does my responsibility grow now that my reach has expanded?”**

Because that’s the real shift. The tools give you the capability to build things that used to require teams. That capability comes with a proportional responsibility to build carefully, ethically, and with genuine regard for the people who will be affected by what you make.

AI is not a replacement for the human spirit. It’s a force multiplier for human intent. The danger isn’t that machines will gain minds of their own — it’s that humans will stop using theirs.

In the age of AI, the only way to become obsolete is to remain stagnant. The only way to be truly successful is to remain human.

## For the Next Cohort: What I Wish Someone Had Told Me

If you’re reading this because you’re considering applying to DEVCON Jumpstart, I want to talk to you directly.

**Come in ready to actually learn.** Not to perform learning — to actually do it. The program will give you exposure to tools and workflows that most CS programs don’t touch yet. Take that seriously.

**Bring your “Contrarian Question.”** What truth do you see that others around you haven’t noticed yet? That perspective is your most valuable asset in any room you walk into. Don’t leave it at the door.

**Hunt for the office secrets.** Find the process that everyone accepts as broken and hasn’t fixed. Fix it. Build the monopoly on that specific value. That’s how you go from being a commodity to being an asset.

**Understand the tools before you exploit them.** Use Claude Code. Use Gemini. Use Google Stitch. Build the hyper-lean pipelines. But understand the mechanics well enough to know when the AI is wrong — because it will be, and catching that is your job, not the AI’s.

**Let the events change you.** Some of the most meaningful parts of this internship happened outside the deliverables — at a hackathon with 300 people, in a conversation with a volunteer who made it to Zurich, in moments where you’re trusted with something real. Show up for those moments fully.

**And whatever you call yourselves** — interns, DEVCON Associates, DEVConnectors — wear it with the understanding that you’re part of something that takes the Philippine tech community seriously enough to invest in its next generation. That’s not nothing. That’s actually quite a lot.
